# Engineering Resume Guide

> Distilled from the r/EngineeringResumes wiki. This is a working reference for reviewing, writing, and tailoring engineering/software resumes. The original wiki states that its guidance is primarily aligned with U.S./Canadian resume conventions and should be treated as general guidance rather than absolute rules.

## 1. Purpose of a Resume

A resume is a short, skimmable pitch whose purpose is to earn an interview. It should quickly communicate:

- who the candidate is
- what relevant technical experience they have
- what they have accomplished
- what engineering skills they can bring to the role

A resume cannot compensate for a lack of genuine relevant experience. Students and early-career engineers should therefore prioritize substantive projects, engineering teams, research, and internships while building their experience.

The governing principle throughout this guide is: **make relevant technical evidence easy to find and easy to understand.**

---

## 2. Core Resume Principles

### Optimize for skimming

Recruiters, hiring managers, and interviewers may initially spend very little time on a resume. Important information should therefore be placed toward the **top and left**, and the document should have a clear visual hierarchy.

### Optimize for substance, not decoration

Use space for evidence that improves candidacy. Avoid decorative elements, unnecessary information, generic claims, and buzzword-heavy descriptions.

### Keep the resume concise

For most students, new graduates, and early/mid-career engineers, target **1 page**. A useful rule of thumb from the wiki is roughly 1 page per decade of experience. A second page is generally more defensible for senior/staff-level candidates with enough relevant material to justify it.

If a resume does not fit on one page, first remove low-value or less-relevant content rather than shrinking everything into an unreadable layout.

### Tailor for the target role

A resume should demonstrate that the candidate can do the specific job being applied for. Reorder and emphasize experience, projects, bullets, and skills based on relevance and impressiveness.

## 3. Layout and Formatting

### Recommended structure

- single-column layout
- clear section separation
- bullets rather than paragraphs
- sufficient white space
- no unnecessary indentation
- no icons, images, or decorative graphics
- readable modern font
- black text with good contrast
- at least roughly 10.5 pt font
- margins of at least roughly 0.4 inches
- line spacing of at least roughly 1.07

Avoid justified text because it creates uneven spacing.

Avoid excessive bold, italics, and ALL CAPS. Do not bold keywords inside bullets. Emphasis should be sparse enough that it actually means something.

### ATS considerations

The resume should be both human-skimmable and readily parsable by an Applicant Tracking System. Favor straightforward text structure over visually complicated layouts.

### Dates

Use consistent date formatting.

Preferred conventions:

- `Present` for an ongoing role/project
- full four-digit years: `2026`, not `'26`
- months written as words or accepted abbreviations, not numeric dates
- if months are used, use them consistently rather than mixing months with seasons/semesters
- no specific day of month
- en dash for ranges: `Jan 2025 – Sept 2026`
- spaces around the en dash
- dates right-aligned to the right margin

Do not let bullet text extend farther right than the dates.

## 4. Section Ordering

Section order depends on career stage.

### Student/new graduate with some work experience

Typical order:

1. Education
2. Work Experience
3. Skills

or, when projects are important:

1. Education
2. Work Experience
3. Projects
4. Skills

### No technical work experience

1. Education
2. Projects
3. Work Experience
4. Skills

### No work experience

1. Education
2. Projects
3. Volunteer Experience / Extracurriculars
4. Skills

### After graduation and establishment in full-time work

Typical order:

1. Work Experience
2. Skills
3. Education

or:

1. Skills
2. Work Experience
3. Education

### Summary/profile

Do **not** normally include a summary/profile for students or ordinary early-career candidates. The wiki considers one more appropriate for:

- senior/staff engineers
- career changers
- candidates explaining an employment gap or return to the workforce

Do not include a references section.

## 5. Contact Information

Keep contact information compact.

### Generally useful

- name
- one professional email address
- GitHub, if it contains worthwhile material
- portfolio/personal website, if worthwhile

### Usually unnecessary or situational

- LinkedIn is considered optional/unnecessary by the wiki
- phone number is considered optional/unnecessary by the wiki
- location should generally be omitted unless being local to the target job is useful
- physical street address and ZIP/postal code should be omitted

If including a phone number, do not prefix it with `Phone:`, `Cell:`, etc.

If including URLs/contact details:

- display them plainly
- do not prefix them with labels such as `Email:` or `GitHub:`
- omit `www.`
- avoid decorative coloring, underlining, or italics
- do not include an empty or neglected GitHub profile

Work authorization/citizenship or security clearance may be included near the name when materially relevant to the hiring situation.

Avoid personal details that could introduce irrelevant bias, including age, gender, children, marital status, ethnicity, religion, and political views.

## 6. Work Experience

Name the section **Experience** or **Work Experience**.

The wiki recommends reserving this section for paid work experience, with research also potentially included. Clearly label internships as internships and contract positions as contract roles.

### What experience bullets should prove

A strong work entry should show:

1. **technical work performed**
2. **technical problems/challenges solved**
3. **impact/results of the work**

Do not turn the resume into a job description. Responsibilities alone do not distinguish a candidate.

Instead of merely saying what the candidate was assigned to do, explain:

- what they built, changed, analyzed, automated, designed, or improved
- why it mattered
- how they solved the technical problem
- relevant engineering decisions or integrations
- what happened as a result

### Prioritize engineering fundamentals for early-career candidates

For new graduates, demonstrate mastery of core engineering/technical work before spending excessive space on project-management or leadership claims. Leadership is more convincing when demonstrated through concrete actions and results rather than asserted abstractly.

### Tools should support the story

Do not make a bullet merely a list of software/tools. The important part is the engineering work accomplished with those tools.

Weak concept:

> Used AWS Lambda, S3, CloudFront, Python, and Playwright

Stronger concept:

> Built an automated evidence-capture pipeline that processed URLs asynchronously, persisted screenshots to S3, and served artifacts through CloudFront

The technologies matter, but **integration and engineering decisions are the story**.

## 7. Bullet-Point Writing

Bullet quality is one of the highest-impact parts of an engineering resume.

### Objectives

Each bullet should ideally communicate some combination of:

- technical work
- technical challenge
- action/implementation
- outcome or impact

### Length and structure

- target 1–2 lines per bullet
- target 1 sentence per bullet
- avoid bullets that wrap onto another line for only a few words
- avoid excessive sub-bullets
- avoid word hyphenation across lines
- order bullets from strongest/most relevant to weakest

### Style

- begin with a strong action verb
- use past tense for completed work
- omit personal pronouns such as `I`, `we`, `our`, etc.
- use digits: `8`, not `eight`
- avoid unnecessary adjectives/adverbs
- avoid ending bullets with periods
- avoid awkward punctuation such as excessive slashes, ampersands, and apostrophes

### Strong action verbs

Examples favored by the wiki include:

- analyzed
- architected
- automated
- built
- created
- decreased
- designed
- developed
- implemented
- improved
- optimized
- published
- reduced
- refactored

### Weak or vague verbs

Avoid verbs that make the candidate sound passive or that fail to communicate the actual contribution, such as:

- aided
- assisted
- coded
- collaborated
- communicated
- exposed to
- gained experience
- helped
- participated
- programmed
- ran
- used
- utilized
- worked on

Also avoid inflated, awkward, or unnecessarily grandiose verbs when a simpler precise verb works better.

## 8. STAR, XYZ, and CAR

The wiki recommends structuring bullets using **STAR**, **XYZ**, or **CAR**.

### STAR

**Situation → Task → Action → Result**

Useful when context/challenge is important to understanding the accomplishment.

Conceptual structure:

> Faced with [situation/problem], [action taken using technical approach], resulting in [outcome]

### CAR

**Challenge → Action → Result**

A compact engineering-friendly structure:

> Solved [challenge] by [technical action], resulting in [result]

### XYZ

**Accomplished X, as measured by Y, by doing Z**

Conceptual structure:

> Improved [X] by [Y] by implementing [Z]

When a defensible metric exists, the wiki recommends moving the metric toward the beginning of the bullet.

### Metrics are valuable, but must be defensible

The source encourages tangible metrics and quantified results when available. It does **not** require fabricating numbers simply to satisfy XYZ.

Good sources of legitimate metrics include:

- latency
- throughput
- uptime
- error rate
- execution/runtime
- infrastructure cost
- deployment frequency/time
- test coverage
- number of users
- number of requests/jobs/items processed
- manual steps eliminated
- team/customer scale
- time saved
- lead time
- budget
- accuracy
- defects/incidents reduced

If no reliable metric exists, write a strong STAR/CAR-style bullet with a concrete outcome rather than inventing a percentage.

## 9. Extracting Strong Bullets from Raw Experience

When raw notes are vague, interrogate the experience before drafting.

### What did you do?

Ask:

- What problem did you solve?
- What feature/system/component did you build?
- Was the work independent or collaborative?
- What was your specific contribution?
- What was the scale?

### How did you do it?

Ask:

- Why was this approach chosen?
- What alternatives or constraints existed?
- What languages, frameworks, cloud services, standards, or tools were involved?
- How did the components interact?
- What testing/validation was performed?
- What failure modes had to be handled?

### What happened because of it?

Ask:

- Did it work in production?
- Who used it?
- How much work did it automate?
- Did it improve reliability, speed, cost, quality, or security?
- Did it reduce manual effort?
- Was it delivered on time or under budget?
- Did it enable another system/team/customer workflow?
- What technical lesson or improvement resulted from problems encountered?

### Draft long, then compress

When experiencing writer's block, first reconstruct the full story without worrying about resume length. Identify the original goal, how it was achieved, and whether the result exceeded the goal. Then compress the strongest technical evidence into a 1–2 line bullet.

## 10. Integration Matters More Than a Parts List

A recurring lesson in the wiki is that engineering resumes should show **how things work together**.

A list of components does not prove much by itself.

Instead of:

> Built system using Python, Lambda, S3, CloudFront, Playwright, and MySQL

Explain the integration:

> Developed a Lambda-driven pipeline that retrieved queued URLs from MySQL, captured evidence through browser/device fallbacks, persisted screenshots to S3, and exposed artifacts through CloudFront

This communicates architecture, flow, and engineering ownership rather than merely keyword coverage.

When writing software bullets, useful questions include:

- What triggered the system?
- Where did data come from?
- How was it processed?
- How did components communicate?
- What happened when the primary path failed?
- Where was state stored?
- How were outputs served or consumed?
- What reliability/security/performance constraints were addressed?

---

## 11. Sensitive or Confidential Work

Do not disclose proprietary, classified, NDA-protected, or otherwise sensitive details.

Instead, describe:

- the type of technical system
- the engineering problem
- the general technologies/standards involved
- the non-sensitive result

Preserve enough technical specificity to demonstrate skill without revealing forbidden implementation/business details.

---

## 12. Education

### Include

- institution
- degree
- graduation date or expected graduation date
- GPA when sufficiently strong/relevant

### Avoid

- high school
- schools from which no degree was received
- degree start dates/date ranges
- ordinary coursework
- unimpressive awards/scholarships

Coursework should generally appear only when unusually specialized and useful.

Use reverse chronological order when multiple degrees exist.

For current students, use a format such as:

> Expected May 2027

For graduates:

> May 2025

or simply:

> 2025

The wiki suggests including GPA when it is notably strong and eventually removing it after establishing a full-time career unless it remains particularly impressive.

## 13. Skills

Name the section simply **Skills**.

### Purpose

The section should provide a compact inventory of technical capabilities relevant to the target role. It is not a database of every technology ever encountered.

### Include

- programming languages the candidate has used substantially and could discuss in an interview
- frameworks/tools/technologies genuinely used
- relevant technical skills appearing elsewhere in the resume

Skills should generally be corroborated by experience/project bullets. If `Python` is listed as an important skill, ideally at least one bullet demonstrates meaningful Python work.

### Do not include

- soft skills such as leadership/teamwork
- typing
- ordinary office software
- IDEs/editors
- operating systems in ordinary cases
- repository hosting sites as skills (`Git`, not `GitHub`)
- proficiency labels such as `Expert`, `Advanced`, or `Professional`

### Formatting

- target 3 lines or fewer
- single-column structure
- group into meaningful categories
- order categories and skills by importance
- comma-separate skills
- capitalize official technology names correctly
- list distinct languages separately (`C, C++`, not `C/C++`)

Possible categories include:

- Languages
- Technologies
- Frameworks
- Cloud
- Databases
- Design Tools
- Simulation and Analysis
- Manufacturing
- Lab Equipment

Do not keep weak skills simply to make the section look larger. A smaller credible list can be stronger than a long list of technologies merely touched once.

---

## 14. Projects

Name the section simply **Projects**.

This section is intended for:

- personal projects
- student design teams
- extracurricular/hobby projects

Do not duplicate work projects here; accomplishments performed for an employer belong under that employer.

### Project formatting

- do not put the word `Project` in every title
- no need to label items `Personal Project`, `Academic Project`, or `Group Project`
- use bullets, not paragraphs
- order projects and bullets by relevance/impressiveness
- roles, locations, and dates are often unnecessary for personal projects
- links to source code or a portfolio can be more valuable

### What makes a project strong

Prefer real, substantive projects over mandatory coursework or copied tutorial projects.

Strong projects tend to:

- solve an actual problem
- have real use, even if the only user is the candidate
- be maintained
- demonstrate engineering decisions
- contain non-trivial integrations
- have working source code/demos where possible

A project's resume bullets should follow the same STAR/CAR/XYZ and technical-impact principles as work-experience bullets.

---

## 15. GitHub and Portfolios

Only include GitHub/portfolio links when they strengthen the application.

A stale or empty profile can consume space without adding value.

### Good repository hygiene

Important projects should ideally have READMEs containing:

- concise project summary
- screenshots where useful
- setup/run instructions
- testing instructions

Automated tests, including unit or end-to-end tests where appropriate, make repositories more credible.

The underlying principle is simple: **do not link to something you would not want the interviewer to inspect.**

---

## 16. Grammar and Language

Poor grammar and typos can signal lack of attention to detail.

### Rules

- run both spelling and grammar checks
- proofread manually
- ask another person to proofread
- capitalize proper nouns correctly
- spell out obscure abbreviations the first time they appear

Example:

> optical character recognition (OCR)

rather than assuming every reader knows an obscure acronym.

For widely understood terms in the target industry, judgment is appropriate.

---

## 17. Senior Engineers and Career Changers

### Senior/staff engineers (roughly 10+ years)

The wiki suggests:

- a brief summary may be useful
- consider separate management and individual-contributor resumes
- try to remain within roughly 2 pages
- discuss influence as well as direct impact
- include relevant organizational/soft achievements alongside business results
- compress older experience
- move Education toward the bottom

### Career changers

A brief 2-sentence summary can provide necessary context about the career transition and motivation.

Also:

- link to working projects/source code
- keep previous unrelated work concise
- aim for a short resume, ideally around 1 page

## 18. Resume Content Priority Framework

When deciding whether something deserves space, rank it roughly by:

1. directly relevant paid technical experience
2. major technical accomplishments within that experience
3. strong projects demonstrating target-role skills
4. technical skills corroborated by experience/projects
5. strong education credentials for students/new graduates
6. unusually impressive extracurricular achievements
7. certifications relevant to the target role
8. everything else

This ranking is a practical distillation of the wiki's repeated emphasis on relevance, technical evidence, accomplishments, and concision; it is not a verbatim ranking supplied by the wiki.

When space is tight, remove low-value material before weakening high-value technical bullets.

## 19. Bullet Quality Test

Before accepting a bullet, check whether a reader can answer most of these questions:

1. **What did the candidate build/change/solve?**
2. **How did they technically do it?**
3. **Why was it needed or what challenge existed?**
4. **What was the outcome?**
5. **Can the outcome be quantified honestly?**
6. **Does this demonstrate a skill relevant to the target job?**
7. **Does it show the candidate's contribution rather than the team's generic work?**
8. **Does it communicate integration/engineering decisions rather than a tool list?**
9. **Can it fit into roughly 1–2 lines?**
10. **Is it stronger than the bullet below it?**

If a bullet only answers “what technology was used?”, it probably needs more work.

## 20. Bullet Transformation Examples

These examples are synthesized to demonstrate the wiki's principles; they are not copied from the wiki.

### Responsibility → accomplishment

Weak:

> Worked on backend APIs using AWS Lambda and MySQL

Better:

> Developed serverless REST APIs with AWS Lambda and MySQL to support [workflow/use case], implementing [important technical mechanism] to achieve [result]

### Tool list → integration

Weak:

> Used Playwright, Telegram, PIL, S3, CloudFront, and Lambda for screenshots

Better:

> Built a fault-tolerant evidence-capture pipeline with Telegram/device and browser fallbacks, storing screenshots in S3 and serving artifacts through CloudFront

### Generic automation → concrete system

Weak:

> Automated reporting forms

Better:

> Automated multi-step abuse-reporting forms by [technical mechanism], eliminating [manual workflow/constraint] and enabling [result]

### Vague OCR → problem and outcome

Weak:

> Used OCR to detect brand misuse

Better:

> Implemented OCR-based brand detection to identify suspected intellectual-property misuse in submitted content, enabling [review/reporting outcome]

### Generic pipeline → technical ownership

Weak:

> Created a code obfuscation pipeline

Better:

> Developed an automated code-obfuscation pipeline that [transformation/process], producing [deployment/security outcome]

The bracketed portions should be filled only with facts that can be defended.

## 21. Metrics: What Counts

Metrics are not limited to revenue or percentages.

For software/cloud engineering, valid measurements may include:

### Performance

- P50/P95/P99 latency
- runtime
- response time
- throughput
- requests per second

### Reliability

- uptime
- error/failure rate
- retry success
- incident count
- recovery time

### Scale

- users
- requests
- records
- URLs
- screenshots
- files
- jobs
- services
- environments
- regions

### Automation

- manual steps eliminated
- hours saved
- workflows automated
- deployment time reduced

### Quality

- test coverage
- bugs/defects reduced
- detection precision/recall/accuracy, when legitimately measured

### Cost

- infrastructure spend
- compute/storage reduction
- resource utilization

### Delivery

- deployment frequency
- lead time
- time-to-release
- project completion against schedule

Use a metric only when the candidate can explain where it came from.

## 22. Common Failure Modes

### Job-description bullets

> Responsible for developing APIs

Problem: describes duty, not achievement.

### Buzzword soup

> Leveraged cutting-edge AWS technologies to strategically enhance scalable cloud solutions

Problem: inflated language with little technical information.

### Technology inventory

> Used Python, AWS, Docker, Terraform, MySQL, Lambda, S3, and CloudFront

Problem: does not explain what was built or how components interacted.

### Unsupported metrics

> Improved efficiency by 80%

Problem: meaningless if the number cannot be explained or defended.

### Generic leadership

> Demonstrated excellent leadership and teamwork

Problem: claims a soft skill instead of proving it through actions.

### Tutorial project

> Built a to-do app following an online React tutorial

Problem: little evidence of independent problem-solving.

### Excessive skills

Listing dozens of technologies can imply shallow exposure rather than competence.

### Over-formatting

Multiple columns, graphics, icons, excessive bolding, tiny fonts, and dense text undermine readability.

## 23. Recommended Resume-Review Workflow

When reviewing or rewriting a resume, use this order:

### Pass 1 — Target

Identify:

- target job family
- seniority
- most important job-description skills
- strongest relevant candidate experiences

### Pass 2 — Structure

Check:

- correct section order for career stage
- one-page target where appropriate
- single-column layout
- clear hierarchy
- consistent dates

### Pass 3 — Content selection

Remove or demote:

- irrelevant content
- duplicated projects/work
- weak coursework
- generic soft skills
- stale links
- weak/trivial projects

### Pass 4 — Bullet reconstruction

For each important experience:

1. identify problem/challenge
2. identify candidate's action
3. identify technical implementation
4. identify result
5. identify legitimate metrics
6. compress into STAR/CAR/XYZ

### Pass 5 — Skills alignment

Ensure skills:

- match the target role
- are genuinely possessed
- appear in supporting bullets where practical
- fit in roughly 3 lines

### Pass 6 — Language

Check:

- strong verbs
- no pronouns
- no fluff
- no unnecessary adjectives/adverbs
- 1 sentence per bullet
- 1–2 lines per bullet
- digits for numbers
- consistent punctuation

### Pass 7 — Proof

Every major claim should be defensible in an interview.

Ask:

> If an interviewer points to this exact bullet and asks “How did you do that?”, can the candidate explain it in technical detail?

If not, rewrite or remove it.
